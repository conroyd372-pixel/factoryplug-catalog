Carbon Fiber Quality Grades: Real vs Fake, Wet vs Dry Layup

Real carbon vs the fakes

Three things get sold as "carbon fiber": real carbon fiber (actual woven carbon fabric in resin), hydro-dipped (a water-transfer print of a carbon pattern on plastic or fiberglass — looks right in photos, zero carbon in it), and vinyl wrap (a sticker). Hydro-dip isn't automatically bad — it's fine for interior trim — but it should never be priced or sold as real carbon. If the listing says "carbon fiber look" or "carbon style," that's your answer.

Wet layup vs dry (prepreg) carbon

Dry carbon (prepreg) is carbon fabric pre-impregnated with resin and cured under pressure in an autoclave — lighter, stronger, and more consistent, but more expensive. Wet layup is resin applied by hand and cured at room temperature — heavier, but affordable. For loaded aero like wings, buy dry; for lips and trim, wet layup is the smart buy.

Wet layup: carbon fabric laid in a mold, resin applied by hand, cured at room temperature or low heat. Affordable, looks great, slightly heavier with more resin — fine for lips, splitters, mirror caps, interior trim. Dry/prepreg carbon: factory-impregnated fabric cured in an autoclave under pressure — lighter, stronger, more consistent, and much more expensive. For aero parts that take load (wings, diffusers), prepreg is worth it. For a front lip, quality wet layup is the smart buy.

Weave patterns: 2x2 twill, plain, forged

2x2 twill is the classic diagonal carbon look — what most people picture. Plain (1x1) weave is a tighter checkerboard. Forged carbon is chopped fibers in resin — the marbled look Lamborghini popularized. None is "better" structurally for street aero; it's aesthetics. What matters is weave consistency — the pattern should run straight and even across the whole part, with no wavy rows or visible patches.

How to spot junk before you buy

  • Ask for close-up photos of the weave, edges, and mounting points — not renders. Real sellers send them.
  • Check the back side. Quality parts are finished (or at least clean) on the back; junk shows raw fiberglass, drips, and rough edges.
  • Mounting points matter most. A lip is only as good as its brackets — ask how it mounts and whether hardware is included.
  • UV clear coat. Carbon yellows in the sun without UV-stable clear. Ask specifically.
  • Weight claims. "50% lighter than stock" with no numbers is marketing. Ask for the actual weight.

Fitment is the real gamble with carbon

Carbon aero is where fitment goes wrong most often — a lip that's 5mm off looks terrible and can't be "adjusted" like metal. Confirm your exact year, model, trim, and bumper variant with the seller (M-sport vs base bumpers are different parts), get it in writing, and check our carbon fiber catalog where every listing already passed the supplier quality bar.

Quick answers

How can I tell real carbon fiber from fake?

Real carbon is woven carbon fabric in resin. Hydro-dip is a printed carbon pattern on plastic or fiberglass, and vinyl is a sticker. If a listing says 'carbon fiber look' or 'carbon style,' it's not real carbon.

What is the difference between wet and dry carbon fiber?

Wet layup is carbon fabric laid in a mold with hand-applied resin — affordable and great for lips, splitters, and trim. Dry (prepreg) carbon is factory-impregnated fabric cured in an autoclave — lighter, stronger, more consistent, and much more expensive. Worth it for loaded aero parts like wings.

What is forged carbon fiber?

Forged carbon is chopped carbon fibers compressed in resin, giving a marbled look popularized by Lamborghini. It's an aesthetic choice — for street aero, weave pattern doesn't decide strength; manufacturing quality does.

How do I check carbon fiber part quality before buying?

Ask for close-up photos (not renders) of the weave, edges, and mounting points. Quality parts have straight consistent weave, finished back sides, solid mounting hardware, and UV-stable clear coat so it won't yellow in the sun.
