What spacers do (and don't do)

A wheel spacer pushes your wheel outward — same effect as a lower offset. It's the cheapest way to fix wheels that sit too tucked, or to clear big brakes. What it doesn't do: change your bolt pattern, fix the wrong center bore, or make an unsafe setup safe. Measure first, buy once.

The 5-minute measuring method

To measure for wheel spacers, park on flat ground with the suspension loaded and hold a straight edge vertically against the fender lip. Measure the gap from the straight edge to the outer face of the tire at all four corners, then subtract 5 mm for suspension travel. That number is your safe spacer thickness — use hub-centric spacers only.

Park on flat ground, wheels straight. Hold a straight edge (a level, a yardstick) vertically against the fender lip, extending down past the wheel. Measure the gap between the straight edge and the outer face of the tire — that's your available poke room. Do it at all four corners (they differ), and do it with the suspension loaded (car on the ground, not on a jack). Subtract 5mm for safety — suspension compresses over bumps, and "flush at rest" becomes "rubbing at speed."

How thick is safe?

3–5mm: subtle, usually fine on stock studs. 10–15mm: the sweet spot for most cars — but check stud engagement (below). 20–25mm: aggressive; use bolt-on style spacers with their own studs. Beyond 25mm: you're changing suspension geometry meaningfully — get the right offset wheels instead. Spacers are a correction tool, not a substitute for correct wheels.

Hub-centric or nothing

Your spacer must be hub-centric: it centers on your car's hub and provides a new hub lip for the wheel to center on. Universal flat spacers leave the wheel hanging on the lugs — vibration, uneven load, bad news. Match the spacer's center bore to your car exactly, and make sure it has the extended hub lip.

The stud rule (this is the safety-critical part)

Your lug nuts/studs need full thread engagement — the rule is at least as many turns as the stud diameter (e.g. a 12mm stud needs ~12 full turns, roughly 7+ turns minimum in practice). Slip-on spacers eat into that engagement. If a 15mm spacer leaves you with 4 turns, you need extended studs or bolt-on spacers. This isn't a style question — a wheel coming off at speed is a catastrophe. When in doubt, go bolt-on.

Torque and maintenance

Torque spacer bolts and lugs to your car's spec with a torque wrench, in a star pattern. Re-torque after 50–100 miles — spacers settle and this step catches it. Check them at every tire rotation. Quality spacers are machined aluminum, anodized, with proper corrosion resistance — not the cheapest cast set with no specs listed.

Quick answers

How do I measure for wheel spacers?

Hold a straight edge vertically against the fender lip and measure the gap to the tire's outer face at all four corners with the car on the ground, then subtract 5mm for suspension travel. That number is your safe spacer thickness.

Are wheel spacers safe?

Quality hub-centric spacers are safe when installed right: correct center bore, full stud thread engagement, torqued to spec, and re-torqued after 50–100 miles. Universal flat spacers and starved stud threads are where spacers get dangerous.

Do I need extended studs with spacers?

If the spacer eats into your lug engagement so the nuts don't get full thread contact, yes — use extended studs or switch to bolt-on style spacers with their own studs. A wheel coming off at speed is catastrophic, so don't guess on this.

How thick can wheel spacers be?

3–5mm is subtle, 10–15mm is the sweet spot for most cars, 20–25mm is aggressive and calls for bolt-on style. Beyond 25mm you're changing suspension geometry — buy correctly offset wheels instead.
