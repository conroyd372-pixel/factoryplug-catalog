How to Pick Coilovers Without Getting Burned

By Free sourcing by RoyblingE90 — free parts sourcing, no gatekeeping.

The short answer

Pick coilovers by their engineering, not their price tag: demand published spring rates (no rates listed is the #1 red flag), check that damping adjustment actually does something, choose monotube for performance or twin-tube for street comfort, and don't reuse worn top mounts. Get an alignment immediately after install — bad toe from lowering eats tires in weeks.

What a coilover actually is

A coilover is a matched spring + shock absorber in one unit, with ride-height adjustment built in. The good ones also let you adjust damping (how stiff the shock is). The bad ones are just a lowering spring bolted to a weak shock with a shiny paint job. Price alone doesn't tell you which is which — the details below do.

Spring rates: the number that decides everything

Spring rate (measured in kg/mm) is how stiff the spring is. Higher = flatter cornering but harsher ride. Street cars typically run 6–10k front / 4–8k rear depending on weight; track cars go higher. Red flag: a kit that won't tell you its spring rates. If the seller can't answer "what are the spring rates?", they didn't engineer anything — walk away.

Damping adjustment: what to actually look for

Single-adjustable (rebound + compression together) is plenty for 95% of drivers. Double-adjustable is for people who tune at the track. What matters more than the click count is whether the adjustment actually does something — on cheap kits, 32 clicks of adjustment might change almost nothing. Ask for a dyno plot if you're spending real money; a serious manufacturer has one.

Monotube vs twin-tube

Monotube shocks handle heat better and respond faster — better for performance driving. Twin-tube rides softer over small bumps and costs less — fine for a street car that just wants a drop. Neither is "better" in absolute terms; match it to how you drive.

Top mounts: don't skip this

The top mount connects the coilover to the car. Pillowball (spherical) mounts give sharper response and usually camber adjustment, but transmit more noise. Rubber OEM-style mounts stay quiet and comfortable. If your kit reuses your 100k-mile stock mounts, budget for new ones — worn mounts ruin new coilovers.

Cheap coilover red flags

  • No published spring rates or damping specs. The #1 tell.
  • Extreme drop claims ("lowers 4 inches!") with no mention of shock travel — bottoming out destroys shocks.
  • No rebuild service. Quality coilovers can be rebuilt; disposable ones can't.
  • Copy-paste listings with the same photos across ten "brands."
  • Price too good to be true for a full set — corners were cut somewhere, usually in the shock internals you can't see.

After you buy: the setup matters more than the brand

Get an alignment immediately — lowering changes camber and toe, and bad toe eats tires in weeks, not months. Corner-balance if you're serious. And break them in gently for the first few hundred miles. Quick answers

What spring rates do I need for coilovers?

It depends on your car's weight and use — street cars typically run 6–10k front and 4–8k rear. The key test: if a seller can't tell you the spring rates, they didn't engineer the kit. Walk away.

Are cheap coilovers worth it?

Usually not. Cheap kits cut corners you can't see — weak shock internals, damping adjustment that does almost nothing, thin flex-prone welds, and no rebuild service. No published spring rates or specs is the #1 red flag.

Monotube vs twin-tube coilovers — which is better?

Monotube shocks handle heat better and respond faster, suiting performance driving. Twin-tube rides softer over small bumps and costs less, suiting a street car that mainly wants a drop. Match it to how you drive.

Do I need an alignment after installing coilovers?

Yes, immediately. Lowering changes camber and toe, and incorrect toe will eat a set of tires in weeks, not months. Get the alignment done the same week you install them.
